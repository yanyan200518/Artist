1. Introduction to Management

Organization — a group of people working together in a structured and coordinated fashion to achieve a set of goals (profit, knowledge, defense, coordination, social satisfaction, etc.)
Management — a set of activities (planning & decision making, organizing, leading, controlling) directed at an organization's resources (human, financial, physical, information) to achieve organizational goals in an efficient and effective manner.
Manager — someone whose primary responsibility is to carry out the management process.
Efficient = using resources wisely, in a cost-effective way.   Effective = making the right decisions and successfully implementing them. Successful orgs are usually both.

The average executive works about 60 hours/week, facing globalization, competition, regulation, shareholder pressure, new tech, and social media.

By Area

  • Marketing managers — get consumers/clients to buy products/services (new product development, promotion, distribution)
  • Financial managers — accounting, cash management, investments
  • Operations managers — create/manage systems that make products/services (production, inventory, quality control, plant layout, site selection)
  • Human resources managers — hiring, recruiting, training, compensation, performance appraisal, discharging employees
  • Administrative/general managers — generalists, not tied to one specialty (e.g., hospital administrator)
  • Specialized: public relations, R&D, internal consultants, international operations managers

3. Basic Management Functions (P-O-L-C)

Planning — setting an organization's goals and deciding how best to achieve them.
Decision making — (part of the planning process) selecting a course of action from a set of alternatives.
Organizing — determining how activities and resources are to be grouped.
Leading — the set of processes used to get members of the organization to work together to further the interests of the organization.
Controlling — monitoring and correcting ongoing activities to facilitate goal attainment.

Key nuance: Managers do NOT perform these strictly in sequence — most move back and forth between them unpredictably (Figure 1.2's dotted vs. solid arrows).

4. Fundamental Management Skills

Technical skills — necessary to accomplish/understand the specific work of the org; most important for first-line managers.
Interpersonal skills — ability to communicate with, understand, and motivate individuals/groups.
Conceptual skills — the ability to think in the abstract; see the organization holistically ("big picture").
Diagnostic skills — ability to visualize the most appropriate response to a situation (like a doctor diagnosing symptoms).
Communication skills — ability to both convey and receive ideas/information effectively.
Decision-making skills — ability to recognize/define problems & opportunities and select the right course of action.
Time management skills — ability to prioritize work, work efficiently, and delegate appropriately.

5. The Science and Art of Management

Science of management — rational, logical, objective, systematic approach; uses data, facts, quantitative models. Best for routine/straightforward problems. Relies on technical, diagnostic, decision-making skills.

Art of management — intuition, experience, instinct, personal insight. Needed because even "objective facts" can be wrong (Starbucks NYC example: research favored drip coffee, but customers wanted espresso).

Effective management = a blend of both science and art.

6. Historical Context of Management

  • Egyptians — used planning, organizing, controlling to build the pyramids
  • Alexander the Great — used a staff organization to coordinate military campaigns
  • Roman Empire — well-defined organizational structure for communication/control
  • Socrates — discussed management practices/concepts, 400 BC
  • Plato — described job specialization, 350 BC
  • al-Farabi (Persian scientist/philosopher) — listed leadership traits, AD 900
  • Robert Owen (1771–1858, British industrialist) — one of the first to recognize importance of human resources & worker welfare
  • Charles Babbage (1792–1871, English mathematician) — focused on production efficiency; believed in division of labor & applying math to facility/material use

Why theory & history matter: theory = conceptual framework for organizing knowledge & guiding action; history helps avoid others' mistakes and provides heritage (e.g., Wells Fargo's corporate historian).

7. The Classical Management Perspective

Classical management perspective — two branches: scientific management + administrative management.

A. Scientific Management (individual worker efficiency)

  • Frederick W. Taylor (1856–1915) — dominant figure; foreman at Midvale Steel; observed "soldiering" (employees deliberately working slower than capable); timed/studied jobs; implemented piecework pay. Worked later at Simonds Rolling Machine Co. and Bethlehem Steel.
  • Steps in Scientific Management (Fig. 1.3): (1) Develop a science for each job element to replace rule-of-thumb methods → (2) Scientifically select & train workers → (3) Supervise to ensure prescribed methods are followed → (4) Continue to plan the work, but let workers do it.
  • Frank & Lillian Gilbreth — husband-wife industrial engineers; bricklaying study reduced movements from 18 to 5, output up ~200%. Wrote/inspired Cheaper by the Dozen (12 children).

B. Administrative Management (managing the total organization)

  • Henri Fayol (1841–1925) — French industrialist; administrative management's most articulate spokesperson; first to identify planning, organizing, leading, controlling as the core managerial functions (work translated to English in 1930).
  • Lyndall Urwick (1891–1983) — British army officer turned theorist/consultant; integrated scientific management with Fayol's work; known for synthesis, not original ideas.
  • Max Weber (1864–1920) — German sociologist; work on bureaucracy (rational guidelines for structuring organizations efficiently); translated to English in 1947.

Limitations of classical perspective: best suited to stable/simple orgs; proposed universal guidelines that don't fit every organization; slighted the role of the individual (→ addressed later by behavioral perspective).

8. The Behavioral Management Perspective

Emphasizes individual attitudes/behaviors and group processes (vs. classical's mechanistic view of workers as "cogs").

  • Hugo Munsterberg (1863–1916) — "father of industrial psychology"; established a psych lab at Harvard, 1892; book Psychology and Industrial Efficiency (translated 1913).
  • Mary Parker Follett (1868–1933) — recognized the human element in the workplace during the scientific management era.

The Hawthorne Studies (1927–1932)

Conducted at Western Electric's Hawthorne plant (near Chicago), sponsored initially by General Electric, led by Elton Mayo (Harvard).

  • Lighting experiment: Productivity rose in BOTH the experimental and control groups — even as lighting was later decreased — because workers received special attention/sympathetic supervision (only declined once lighting hit "moonlight" level, and GE withdrew sponsorship).
  • Incentive-pay experiment (9 men, terminal banks): The group informally set its own acceptable output level. Overproducers = "rate busters"; underproducers = "chiselers."
  • Conclusion: social/individual processes matter more than previously thought in shaping worker behavior.

Human Relations Movement

Workers respond primarily to the social context of the workplace. Manager's concern for workers → increased satisfaction → improved performance.

  • Abraham Maslow (1908–1970) — 1943, hierarchy of needs theory.
  • Douglas McGregor (1906–1964) — Theory X / Theory Y (1960).
Theory X (pessimistic — like scientific mgmt)Theory Y (positive — human relations view)
People dislike work, avoid itWork is a natural part of life; not disliked
Must be controlled, directed, coerced, threatenedInternally motivated to reach committed objectives
Prefer direction, avoid responsibility, little ambition, want securitySeek & accept responsibility under favorable conditions; capable of innovation; often underutilized potential

McGregor believed Theory Y was the better philosophy for managers.

Organizational behavior — contemporary, more realistic field acknowledging human behavior in orgs is more complex than human relationists believed; interdisciplinary (psychology, sociology, anthropology, economics, medicine). Covers job satisfaction, stress, motivation, leadership, group dynamics, organizational politics, conflict, structure/design.

9. The Quantitative Management Perspective

Emerged during World War II — mathematical approaches (originally from Taylor/Gantt) applied to military logistics; later adopted by firms like DuPont & GE.

Management science — focuses specifically on developing mathematical models (a simplified representation of a system/process/relationship).
Operations management — a form of "applied" management science; less mathematically sophisticated; helps orgs produce products/services more efficiently (inventory management, linear programming, queuing theory, break-even analysis, simulation).

Limitations: can't fully account for individual behavior/attitudes; time spent on quantitative technique can slow development of other skills; models require assumptions that may be unrealistic.

10. Contemporary Perspectives: The Systems Perspective

System — an interrelated set of elements functioning as a whole.

Four basic elements (Figure 1.4):

  1. Inputs — material, human, financial, information resources from the environment
  2. Transformation process — technology, operating systems, administrative systems
  3. Outputs — products/services, profits/losses, employee behaviors, information
  4. Feedback — environment's reaction back to the system
  • Open systems — interact with their environment. Closed systems — do not (organizations are open systems; ignoring the environment is a mistake).
  • Subsystems — systems within a broader system (e.g., marketing, production, finance depts within a firm); interdependent — a change in one affects others.
  • Synergy — subsystems working together produce more than working alone (e.g., Disney's movies + theme parks + merchandise; P&G–Gillette acquisition).
  • Entropy — a normal process leading to system decline if an org fails to monitor feedback and adapt (e.g., Studebaker, Circuit City went bankrupt).

11. The Contingency Perspective

Universal perspectives (classical, behavioral, quantitative) try to find the "one best way" to manage.
Contingency perspective — appropriate managerial behavior in a given situation depends ("is contingent") on the unique elements of that situation — no universal theory fits every organization.

Example: Cisco CEO John Chambers shifted from an autocratic, command-and-control style to a more democratic style after the 2001 tech crash/downsizing — illustrating that management style must adapt to the situation.

12. Contemporary Management Issues & Challenges

  • Globalization — varying property ownership, resources, government roles, and cultural/behavioral norms across countries.
  • Ethics & social responsibility — corporate governance, business scandals (e.g., Allen Stanford's ~$7B Ponzi scheme), environmental/social obligations.
  • Quality — basis for competition (e.g., Lexus/J.D. Power); improves productivity, lowers costs (Whistler Corp. used 100 of 250 employees just to repair defective units).
  • Shift to a service economy — service sector was <50% of U.S. GNP in 1947, 65% by 1975, over 80% by 2010; ~90% of new U.S. jobs since 1990 are service-sector.
  • Economic recession (2008–2010) & slow recovery (2011–2014) — cost-cutting, streamlining, some firms positioned for renewed growth.
  • The changing/rapidly evolving workplace — workforce reduction & expansion cycles.
  • Workforce diversity — differences in age, gender, ethnicity, physical abilities; ~60% of U.S. women 16+ are now in the workforce (vs. ~1/3 fifty years ago).
  • Generational shifts — 1980s "yuppies" (career-devoted) vs. Gen X, Gen Y, Millennials (favor flexibility, individuality, lifestyle/location over long-term corporate loyalty).
  • Organization change — orgs that fail to monitor environment & adapt risk failure; some managers deliberately "shake things up" to avoid complacency.
  • Technology — smartphones, wireless networks, social media ease communication but increase work pace, reduce time for contemplation, raise employee privacy issues.
