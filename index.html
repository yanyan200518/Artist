1. Moral Dilemma — The Basics

Dilemma — a situation where a person is forced to choose between two or more conflicting options, neither of which is acceptable.
Moral (ethical) dilemma — a dilemma involving human actions with moral implications; a "moral agent" is forced to choose between two or more conflicting options, none of which resolves the situation in a morally acceptable way.

Key distinction: If a person faces a hard situation but is NOT forced to choose between conflicting options, they are not in a dilemma — just a problematic/distressful situation, and the logical move is to look for solutions.

Example (Efreminda): A deeply religious woman who believes killing is wrong has an ectopic pregnancy. Continuing the pregnancy endangers her life; abortion would save her life but violate her moral conviction. Two conflicting options, neither fully acceptable.

Karen Allen's 3 Conditions for a Moral Dilemma

  1. The moral agent is obliged to decide which course of action is best.
  2. There must be different courses of action to choose from.
  3. Some moral principle is always compromised, no matter the choice — there is no perfect solution. Benjiemen Labastin: the agent "seems fated to commit something wrong" — by choosing one moral requirement, they necessarily fail another.

2. Types of Moral Dilemmas

The text groups common types into four pairs:

TypeDefinitionExample
EpistemicTwo+ requirements conflict and the agent doesn't know which takes precedence — but with fuller knowledge, one option WOULD be better.Promised to be home early for your son, but see a sick old man needing a hospital on the way.
OntologicalTwo+ requirements conflict and neither overrides the other — genuinely, equally weighted; not a knowledge problem.Military doctor, two wounded soldiers, only one bag of blood.
Self-imposedCaused by the moral agent's own wrongdoing/choices.David, a mayoral candidate, promises indigenous peoples he'll protect their forest, but also takes funding from a mining corporation.
World-imposedExternal events place the agent in moral conflict — not the agent's own doing.Sophie's Choice (see below).
ObligationMore than one feasible action is obligatory."Sartre's Student" (a student torn between duty to join the war and duty to care for his mother).
ProhibitionAll feasible actions are forbidden.Sophie's Choice is the classic example.
Single-agentOne agent "ought to do A" AND "ought to do B," but cannot do both.A doctor whose patient has HIV: legal duty to report vs. desire to respect confidentiality.
Multi-personSeveral people (family, org, community) must each act on their own obligations, which conflict — requires more than "doing right," it needs consensus.A family deciding to terminate or prolong a member's life; a company choosing between cutting staff or underpaying to comply with wage law.
Sophie's Choice (William Styron): Sophie Zawistowska is forced by an SS doctor (Fritz Jemand von Niemand) to choose which of her two children — Eva or Jan — will be sent to the gas chamber at Auschwitz. If she refuses to choose, both die. She chooses Eva; Jan is sent to the Children's Camp. Used as the classic example of BOTH a world-imposed dilemma and a prohibition dilemma.

3. Freedom as the Foundation of Moral Acts

Freedom — the power or right to act, speak, or think as one wants without hindrance or restraint. It also stands for securing everyone an equal opportunity for life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.

Freedom is NOT total license ("do whatever I want" = anarchy). Your freedom to act ends where it starts infringing on another person's freedom (e.g., you can't freely shout "Fire!" in a crowded theater).

"Freedom of/to" vs. "Freedom from"

President Franklin Delano Roosevelt's Four Freedoms (State of the Union, January 6, 1941):

  1. Freedom of speech and expression
  2. Freedom of worship
  3. Freedom from want (economic security for all)
  4. Freedom from fear (reduction of armaments/aggression)

Securing "freedom from" fear and want usually requires collective, organized action — typically government. Liberal representative democracy is presented as doing this best. FDR also framed the U.S. as part of an interdependent "family of nations."

FDR's basic foundations of a healthy democracy: equality of opportunity, jobs for those who can work, security for those who need it, ending special privilege for the few, preserving civil liberties, and enjoying the fruits of scientific progress.

4. Culture and Its Role in Moral Behavior

Culture — a way of life; the totality of the social environment in which we conduct our lives. We don't choose the culture we're born into, but we can grow and adjust to it.

Our morality is shaped both by the culture acquired at home and by social experience — home, school, community, church, society, friends, relatives, entertainment, news, and social media all leave an imprint. The type of media we consume influences how we see ourselves, others, and morality.

5. Cultural Relativism

Holmes (1998) — three theses: (1) customs, practices, and moral beliefs vary from culture to culture; (2) morality depends on human nature, human condition, and specific social/cultural circumstances; (3) rightness/wrongness may vary from person to person and culture to culture — this last point is relativism.

Cultural relativism — the view that a particular behavior may be judged morally right if one's own culture approves of it. Morality is relative to culture.

Implication: Cultures "cannot be wrong" and cannot be legitimately criticized for their moral judgments; individuals who question their own culture's morality (from within this view) can never be considered "morally right." When different cultures make different judgments about the same act, both may be considered correct.

Why many oppose it: If right and wrong are purely relative, people might abandon self-regulation entirely; moral responsibility and moral progress become impossible; there's no way to say which culture's answer to a moral question is the "proper" one, since we can't step outside all cultures to judge.

6. The Filipino Way

Filipino culture shares similarities with Asian neighbors and was shaped by foreign rulers for over 300 years.

Religious Orientation

Filipinos are part of a nation with strong religious inclination; monotheism dominates. Morality and values are strongly tied to religious beliefs, tenets, and rites. Yet there's often a gap between religious devotion and actual behavior — Fr. Jaime Bulatao (1960) coined the term "split-level" Christians to describe this inconsistency (pious in church, but irresponsible/corrupt elsewhere).

Perception of How Others Judge Us

Fr. Vitaliano Gorospe, S.J. (1966): the Filipino conscience is more external than internal — judgments of good/bad, right/wrong are shaped more by the presence and opinion of others than by internal conviction. This is captured in the concept of "hiya" (roughly, shame/sense of social propriety). Example from the text: a mother restricts her daughter's clothing not because she values decency itself, but because of what the neighbors might think of her as a mother.

Conclusion (Boylan, 2000): Our view of morality is unique to each of us because of (1) the biological characteristics handed down by our parents, and (2) the unique upbringing and historical/social circumstances we each experienced.

7. Kohlberg's Stages of Moral Development

The Heinz Dilemma: Heinz's wife is dying of cancer. A chemist has discovered a drug that could save her but charges 10x its production cost. Heinz can only raise half the money; the chemist refuses to lower the price or delay payment. Heinz breaks in and steals the drug. (Kohlberg used people's reasoning about this story to build his theory.)

Kohlberg identified 3 levels of moral reasoning, each with 2 sub-stages (6 stages total). People pass through them in order; reasoning from an earlier stage gets replaced by the new one. Not everyone reaches every stage.

LevelWhoBasis of ReasoningStages
1. Pre-conventionalMost 9-year-olds & youngerPhysical consequences of actions; authority is external Stage 1 — Obedience & Punishment: good to avoid punishment.

Stage 2 — Individualism & Exchange: recognizes different viewpoints exist besides authority's.
2. ConventionalMost adolescents & adultsNorms of one's group; authority internalized but not questioned Stage 3 — Good Interpersonal Relationships: good to be seen as good by others; approval-seeking.

Stage 4 — Maintaining the Social Order: obey laws/rules to uphold order, avoid guilt.
3. Post-conventionalOnly ~10-15% of people reach itSelf-chosen principles; individual rights and justice Stage 5 — Social Contract & Individual Rights: laws are for the good of the majority, but can work against individuals.

Stage 6 — Universal Principles: own moral guidelines (human rights, justice, equality) that may not match the law; will defend them even against society.

8. Feelings and Moral Decision Making

We like to think our moral decisions are rational/impartial, but we are "feeling beings" — our feelings (moral emotions) are always present and matter. Experts believe it is impossible to make important moral judgments without emotions.

Emotion typeExamplesEffect
Inner-directed negativeGuilt, embarrassment, shameMotivate the person to act ethically
Outer-directed negativeAnger, disgust, contemptAim to discipline/punish others who acted unethically; discourages others from repeating it
PositiveGratitude, admirationFelt when seeing compassion/kindness; prompts people to help others
Suffering-basedSympathy, empathyLead people to act ethically toward others; empathy is the central moral emotion that most commonly motivates prosocial activity.

Bottom line: Even though we tend to credit philosophy or religious values for our moral decisions, emotions play a significant, often underestimated, role.

9. The Moral Psychology of Thomas Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas — a Dominican priest and philosopher (over 700 years ago) who explored HOW humans think, feel, and choose in moral situations, rather than just listing rules.

Human Action in Three Parts

  1. Intellect — our power to reason and understand; recognizes general truths ("cheating is wrong") and applies them to specific situations; evaluates options and searches for what is truly good.
  2. Will — our ability to make free choices. Even if the intellect knows what's right, it's the will that actually chooses it — and sometimes the will chooses what merely seems good rather than what is truly good.
  3. Passions — emotions/bodily responses (fear, compassion, anxiety). Aquinas does NOT see passions as inherently bad — when guided by reason, they can support moral action.

Framework for Moral Reasoning

Synderesis — our natural, innate grasp of basic moral truths ("do good and avoid evil"); a built-in moral compass that operates automatically.
Conscience — applies the general truths known through synderesis to a SPECIFIC situation, through practical reasoning.
Natural Law — the moral order written into human nature itself; through reason, we can discover what leads to human flourishing. It's the OBJECTIVE foundation.

How they work together: Synderesis provides the initial moral awareness -> Conscience applies it through deliberate reasoning about a specific case -> Natural Law supplies the objective foundation that validates and guides the whole process.
