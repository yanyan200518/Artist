Chapter 1 - Understanding the Manager's Job

Core Definitions

  • Organization: a coordinated group of people working toward goals.
  • Management: activities directed at resources to achieve goals efficiently and effectively.
  • Manager: person primarily responsible for carrying out the management process.
  • Efficient: using resources wisely and cost-effectively.
  • Effective: choosing and implementing the right actions.

Manager Types by Level

  • Top managers: overall organization, strategy, policy, external representation.
  • Middle managers: implement top plans and coordinate lower managers.
  • First-line managers: supervise operating employees and daily work.

Manager Types by Area

  • Marketing: product, promotion, distribution, customer demand.
  • Financial: accounting, cash, investments, financial resources.
  • Operations: production systems, inventory, quality, layout, site selection.
  • Human resources: staffing, training, compensation, appraisal, separation.
  • Administrative/general: broad familiarity across areas, no single specialty.
  • Specialized: public relations, R&D, internal consulting, international operations, and similar roles.

The Management Process

Planning and decision makingSet goals and select courses of action from alternatives.
OrganizingDetermine how activities and resources should be grouped.
LeadingGet people to work together in the organization's interest.
ControllingMonitor progress and correct activities to reach goals.

Exam trap: the functions are presented in a logical order, but real managers move among them constantly.

Fundamental Management Skills

Technical

Know how to perform or understand specific work. Most critical for first-line managers.

Interpersonal

Communicate with, understand, and motivate individuals and groups inside and outside the firm.

Conceptual

Think abstractly, see the whole organization, and understand how parts fit together.

Diagnostic

Analyze symptoms, identify likely causes, and choose an appropriate response.

Communication

Send and receive ideas accurately through speech, writing, listening, and interpretation.

Decision-making

Recognize problems and opportunities, then choose a suitable course of action.

Time management

Prioritize, work efficiently, and delegate appropriately.

Management as Science and Art

  • Science: rational, objective, systematic, data-driven methods; especially useful for routine or measurable issues.
  • Art: intuition, experience, insight, judgment; especially important when facts are incomplete or alternatives look equally attractive.
  • Best answer: effective management blends both.

History and Theory

  • Theory: a conceptual framework for organizing knowledge and guiding action.
  • Why theory matters: helps managers interpret situations instead of improvising blindly.
  • Why history matters: gives heritage, context, and warnings against repeating mistakes.
  • Early history: Egyptians used planning, organizing, and controlling for pyramids; Greeks, Romans, and earlier thinkers discussed specialization, leadership, and structure.
  • Robert Owen: stressed the importance and welfare of human resources.
  • Charles Babbage: emphasized production efficiency, division of labor, and mathematical analysis.

Classical Management Perspective

Scientific Management

Focuses on improving individual worker efficiency. Key figure: Frederick W. Taylor.

  • Taylor observed soldiering: workers deliberately working below capability.
  • Core steps: develop a science for each job element, scientifically select and train employees, supervise prescribed methods, and separate planning from doing.
  • Frank and Lillian Gilbreth studied motions and inefficiencies, including bricklaying; Lillian also influenced industrial psychology and personnel management.

Administrative Management

Focuses on the total organization rather than individual jobs.

  • Henri Fayol: identified planning, organizing, leading, and controlling as core managerial functions.
  • Lyndall Urwick: integrated and synthesized earlier administrative ideas.
  • Max Weber: developed ideas about bureaucracy and rational structure.

Classical strengths: efficiency, job design, bureaucracy, management as a field of study. Limits: assumed stable/simple organizations, overused universal rules, and slighted the human side.

Behavioral Management Perspective

  • Emphasizes individual attitudes, behaviors, group processes, and human needs.
  • Hugo Munsterberg: father of industrial psychology; linked psychology to selection and motivation.
  • Mary Parker Follett: anticipated the human side of management and the importance of behavior in organizations.
  • Hawthorne studies: Mayo and associates found that attention, social acceptance, and group norms affected productivity.
  • Human relations movement: argued that workers respond strongly to social context, group norms, and interpersonal dynamics.
  • Maslow: hierarchy of needs influenced motivation theory.
  • McGregor's Theory X: negative view; people avoid work, need control, avoid responsibility.
  • McGregor's Theory Y: positive view; work can be natural, people seek responsibility, are creative, and can self-direct when committed.
  • Organizational behavior: modern, interdisciplinary field studying individual, group, and organizational processes.

Quantitative Management Perspective

  • Emerged strongly during World War II to allocate military resources more efficiently.
  • Focuses on quantitative techniques, decision making, cost-effectiveness, mathematical models, and computers.
  • Management science: develops mathematical models of systems, processes, or relationships.
  • Operations management: applied management science aimed at producing products or services efficiently; includes inventory, scheduling, linear programming, queuing, break-even analysis, and simulation.
  • Limitation: models rely on assumptions and cannot fully capture attitudes and individual behavior.

Contemporary Perspectives

Systems Perspective

  • A system is an interrelated set of elements functioning as a whole.
  • Basic flow: inputs from environment, transformation process, outputs to environment, feedback.
  • Open system: interacts with environment. Organizations are open systems.
  • Closed system: does not interact with environment.
  • Subsystems: systems within the larger system, such as marketing or finance.
  • Synergy: units produce more together than separately.
  • Entropy: system decline if the organization fails to renew and adjust.

Contingency Perspective

  • Rejects the idea of one universal best way.
  • The appropriate managerial action depends on the situation's unique elements.
  • Exam clue: if a question says "it depends," think contingency.

Modern Challenges

  • Globalization: different ownership patterns, infrastructure, cultures, work norms, and decision styles.
  • Ethics, social responsibility, and corporate governance.
  • Quality as a basis of competition and productivity improvement.
  • Service economy: intangible outputs and time/place utility; service firms still use many operations tools.
  • Economic downturns and recovery: cost reduction, restructuring, and strategy refinement.
  • New workplace: diversity, generational differences, flexibility, lifestyle preferences, and changing motivation patterns.
  • Technology and social media: faster communication, more information, privacy issues, and accelerated work pace.

Chapter 3 - Planning and Strategic Management

Planning Process

  • Planning occurs inside an environmental context; managers must understand that context first.
  • The mission states purpose, premises, values, and direction.
  • Mission leads to strategic goals and strategic plans.
  • Strategic goals and plans shape tactical goals and tactical plans.
  • Tactical goals and plans shape operational goals and operational plans.
  • Goals and plans at each level feed future planning.

Purposes of Goals

  • Provide guidance and unified direction.
  • Facilitate planning.
  • Motivate and build commitment.
  • Support evaluation and control.

Goal Levels

  • Mission: fundamental purpose and scope.
  • Strategic goals: broad top-management goals.
  • Tactical goals: middle-management goals that operationalize strategy.
  • Operational goals: lower-level, short-term goals tied to tactical goals.

Plan Levels

  • Strategic plans: resource allocation, priorities, and action steps to reach strategic goals.
  • Tactical plans: implement specific parts of strategic plans.
  • Operational plans: carry out tactical plans for operational goals.

Strategic Management

StrategyA comprehensive plan for accomplishing organizational goals.
Strategic managementOngoing process of formulating and implementing effective strategies.
Effective strategyAligns organization and environment while achieving strategic goals.
Distinctive competenceAn organizational strength possessed by only a small number of competitors.
ScopeThe markets in which the organization will compete.
Resource deploymentHow the organization distributes resources across areas of competition.
  • Business-level strategy: how an organization competes in a particular industry or market.
  • Corporate-level strategy: how an organization manages operations across several industries or markets.
  • Strategy formulation: deciding the content of strategy, or what the strategy is.
  • Strategy implementation: operationalizing and executing the strategy, or how it is achieved.

SWOT Analysis

  • Strengths: internal skills and capabilities that enable strategy.
  • Weaknesses: internal limitations that hinder strategy.
  • Opportunities: external areas that may generate higher performance.
  • Threats: external areas that make high performance harder.
  • Good strategy should support the mission, exploit strengths and opportunities, neutralize threats, and avoid or correct weaknesses.

Business-Level Strategy

Differentiation

Distinguish products or services through quality, image, service, features, or value customers will pay extra for.

Overall Cost Leadership

Gain advantage by keeping costs below competitors and selling profitably at low prices.

Focus

Concentrate on a region, product line, or buyer group; can use either cost or differentiation within that niche.

Product Life Cycle

  • Introduction: new product or technology; priority is getting product out without sacrificing quality.
  • Growth: more competitors enter; quality, delivery, differentiation, and barriers to entry matter.
  • Maturity: demand growth slows; firms may exit; cost control and new product search become important.
  • Decline: demand and number of producers fall; firms need differentiation, low costs, or new products to survive.

Corporate-Level Strategy

  • Strategic business unit (SBU): a business or set of businesses within a larger organization.
  • Diversification: number of businesses and the extent to which they are related.
  • Single-product strategy: one product/service in one geographic market; focused but risky if the product fails.
  • Related diversification: multiple linked businesses; reduces dependence, spreads overhead, and exploits synergy.
  • Unrelated diversification: unrelated businesses; theoretically stabilizes performance and allocates resources, but often underperforms because managers lack deep knowledge and do not exploit synergy.

BCG Matrix

  • Dogs: low share, low growth; avoid investment or divest.
  • Cash cows: high share, low growth; generate funds for other businesses.
  • Question marks: low share, high growth; invest carefully or divest if performance disappoints.
  • Stars: high share, high growth; invest to maintain strong position.

GE Business Screen

  • Uses industry attractiveness and competitive position.
  • Classifies businesses as winners, losers, question marks, average businesses, or profit producers.
  • Industry attractiveness includes market growth, market size, capital requirements, and competitive intensity.
  • Competitive position includes market share, technological know-how, product quality, service network, price competitiveness, and operating costs.

Tactical and Operational Planning

  • Tactical plans implement specific parts of strategy; strategy is about the war, tactics are about battles.
  • Tactical planning should flow from strategy, specify resources and time frames, and require human resource commitments.
  • Execution depends on evaluating actions against goals, giving decision makers information and resources, coordinating vertically and horizontally, and monitoring results.
  • Single-use plans: for actions unlikely to recur. A program is large and complex; a project is narrower.
  • Standing plans: for recurring activities. Policies are broad guides; SOPs list steps; rules and regulations specify exact behavior and can replace decision making.
  • Contingency planning: alternative courses if intended plans are disrupted.
  • Crisis management: procedures for disasters or unexpected calamities.
  • Contingency planning action points: develop plan and consider events; implement plan and identify events; set indicators and contingency plans; complete original or contingency plan.

Chapter 8 - Managing Human Resources in Organizations

HRM Context and Strategic Importance

  • Human resource management (HRM): activities directed at attracting, developing, and maintaining an effective workforce.
  • HRM matters strategically because people affect productivity, legal exposure, costs, morale, and performance.
  • Human capital: the organization's investment in attracting, retaining, and motivating an effective workforce.
  • HR departments and line managers share responsibility: HR may design systems and screen applicants, while line managers often make final selection, appraisal, and coaching decisions.

Legal Environment of HRM

Equal Employment Opportunity

  • Title VII: forbids discrimination based on sex, race, color, religion, or national origin in the employment relationship.
  • Adverse impact: protected groups pass a selection standard at less than 80 percent of the majority group's pass rate.
  • EEOC: enforces Title VII and related employment laws.
  • Age Discrimination in Employment Act: protects people older than 40.
  • Affirmative action: intentionally seeking and hiring qualified or qualifiable underrepresented workers; required for certain government contractors.
  • Pregnancy Discrimination Act: forbids pregnancy-based discrimination.
  • Americans with Disabilities Act: forbids disability discrimination and requires reasonable accommodation.
  • Civil Rights Act of 1991: makes discrimination suits easier while limiting punitive damages.

Compensation, Benefits, Labor, Safety

  • Fair Labor Standards Act: minimum wage and overtime after 40 hours for covered workers.
  • Equal Pay Act: men and women must receive equal pay for the same job.
  • ERISA: regulates pension fund management and security.
  • Family and Medical Leave Act: up to 12 weeks unpaid leave for family and medical emergencies.
  • National Labor Relations Act/Wagner Act: union election procedures and collective bargaining requirements.
  • NLRB: enforces Wagner Act provisions.
  • Taft-Hartley Act: limits union power and defines management rights during organizing campaigns.
  • OSHA: requires safe work conditions and compliance with safety and health standards.

Emerging legal issues: sexual harassment, alcohol and drug abuse, and AIDS/disability-related protections.

Attracting Human Resources

Human Resource Planning

  • Job analysis: systematic collection of job information.
  • Job description: duties, working conditions, tools, materials, equipment.
  • Job specification: skills, abilities, and credentials needed.
  • Forecast demand using past HR use, future plans, sales forecasts, and economic trends.
  • Forecast internal and external labor supply.
  • Replacement chart: key managerial jobs, incumbents, likely tenure, and possible successors.
  • Skills inventory: employee education, skills, experience, and career goals.

Recruiting

  • Recruiting: attracting qualified applicants.
  • Internal recruiting: considers current employees; boosts morale and retention but creates ripple effects.
  • External recruiting: ads, campus interviews, agencies, search firms, referrals, walk-ins, online postings.
  • Realistic job preview: shows the job honestly so new hires are less likely to quit from unmet expectations.

Selection

  • Validation: proving a selection device predicts future job performance.
  • Application blanks gather work history, education, and job-related data.
  • Tests can measure ability, skill, aptitude, knowledge, intelligence, or personality if job-related and validated.
  • Structured interviews improve consistency and validity.
  • Assessment centers simulate managerial work and use trained observers.
  • Other tools include physical exams, drug tests, credit checks, and limited use of polygraphs.

Developing Human Resources

  • Training: teaches operational or technical employees how to do current jobs.
  • Development: teaches managers and professionals skills for present and future jobs.
  • Training should begin with needs assessment; not every poor performance problem is a training problem.
  • Training goals should be specific and measurable; evaluation should focus on whether job performance improved, not only whether participants liked the program.
  • Methods depend on content: reading or lectures for facts, role play or cases for interpersonal skills, on-the-job or vestibule training for physical skills, web-based tools for flexible delivery.

Performance Appraisal and Feedback

Why Appraise?

  • Validate selection devices.
  • Assess training impact.
  • Make administrative decisions on pay, promotion, and training.
  • Give feedback and support career planning.

Methods

  • Objective measures: output, scrap rate, sales, claims processed.
  • Opportunity bias: some employees have better chances to perform than others.
  • Special performance test: standardized conditions to measure ability.
  • Ranking: compares employees from best to worst.
  • Rating: compares each employee with a fixed standard.
  • BARS: rating scale with specific behavioral anchors.

Common Errors

  • Recency error: overweights recent behavior.
  • Leniency, severity, or central tendency errors.
  • Halo error: one strong dimension spills into other ratings.
  • Intentional or unintentional discrimination.
  • 360-degree feedback: boss, peers, and subordinates evaluate the manager.

Feedback should be private, fact-focused, and aimed at improvement. Managers often need training to deliver negative feedback constructively.

Maintaining Human Resources

  • Compensation: financial remuneration for work.
  • Wages: hourly pay for operating employees.
  • Salary: pay for overall contributions rather than hours worked.
  • Incentives: performance-linked compensation such as commissions or bonuses.
  • Wage-level decision: whether to pay above, at, or below market rates.
  • Wage structure decision: job evaluation establishes job worth relative to other jobs.
  • Individual wage decision: pay for a particular person based on seniority, qualifications, merit, or a combination.
  • Benefits: noncash or indirect compensation such as leave, insurance, pensions, workers' compensation, Social Security, tuition reimbursement, and recreation.
  • Cafeteria benefit plan: basic coverage plus employee choice among additional benefits within a cost limit.

Workforce Diversity

  • Diversity exists when group members differ along important dimensions such as gender, age, ethnicity, ability, family status, and other demographic traits.
  • Competitive advantages: higher productivity, lower turnover and absenteeism, better applicant attraction, better market understanding, more creativity and innovation.
  • Conflict sources: perceptions of unfair advantage, misunderstanding, inappropriate interaction, fear, distrust, prejudice, or resistance to difference.
  • Individual strategies: understanding, tolerance, empathy, and communication.
  • Organizational approaches: fair policies, clear responses to problems, flexible practices, diversity and multicultural training, language support, and a tolerant culture.

Labor Relations and New Challenges

Union Formation

  • Employees develop interest in unionization.
  • At least 30 percent of the bargaining unit signs authorization cards before the NLRB holds an election.
  • Bargaining unit: employees eligible to vote and be represented.
  • Secret ballot election; simple majority of those voting certifies the union.
  • After certification, the union signs up members, elects officers, and negotiates a first contract.
  • Decertification election can remove union representation.

Collective Bargaining

  • Management and union negotiate a labor contract.
  • Contract topics include wages, hours, security, promotion, layoffs, discipline, benefits, overtime, vacations, rest periods, and grievances.
  • A grievance procedure enforces the contract.
  • Final unresolved grievances go to binding arbitration by a labor law expert.

Changing Workplace

  • Knowledge workers: add value through what they know; often identify strongly with a profession and need continual training.
  • Contingent workers: work on something other than a permanent full-time basis, including contractors, on-call workers, temps, leased employees, and part-timers.
  • Managers must plan contingent work, understand real costs and limits, and integrate temporary workers consistently.
