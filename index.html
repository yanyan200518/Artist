CHAPTER 2 — The Environments of Organizations and Managers

Fundamentals of Management, 8th Edition — Griffin  |  Reviewer + 78-Item Quiz

Learning Outcomes

  1. Discuss the nature of an organization's environments and identify the components of its general, task, and internal environments.
  2. Describe the ethical and social environment of management, including individual ethics, the concept of social responsibility, and how organizations can manage social responsibility.
  3. Discuss the international environment of management, including trends in international business, levels of international business activities, and the context of international business.
  4. Describe the importance and determinants of an organization's culture, as well as how organizational culture can be managed.

I. The Organization's Environments

External environment — everything outside an organization's boundaries that might affect it. It has two separate parts: the general environment and the task environment.
Internal environment — the conditions and forces within the organization.

A. The General Environment

The set of broad dimensions and forces in an organization's surroundings that determine its overall context. Three dimensions covered in this chapter:

Economic dimension — the overall health and vitality of the economic system in which the organization operates (growth, inflation, interest rates, unemployment).
Technological dimension — the methods available for converting resources into products or services.
Political–legal dimension — the government regulation of business and the relationship between business and government.
Key fact — The chapter's "Tough Times, Tough Choices" feature ties the economic dimension to the business cycle: the U.S. economy fell into recession beginning in 2008 and only began to recover in 2012, with the stock market hitting an all-time high by early 2013.
Key fact — Political–legal dimension is important for three reasons: (1) the legal system defines what an organization can/cannot do, (2) pro- or anti-business sentiment in government influences business activity, (3) political stability affects planning.

B. The Task Environment

Because the general environment's impact is often vague, imprecise, and long term, organizations focus more on the task environment — specific organizations or groups that affect the organization: competitors, customers, suppliers, strategic partners, and regulators.

Competitor — an organization that competes with other organizations for resources.
Customer — whoever pays money to acquire an organization's products or services.
Supplier — an organization that provides resources for other organizations.
Regulator — a body that has the potential to control, legislate, or otherwise influence an organization's policies and practices. Two kinds: regulatory agencies (created by government, e.g., EPA, SEC, FDA, EEOC) and interest groups (organized by members to influence organizations, e.g., NOW, MADD, NRA, Sierra Club).
Strategic partners (strategic allies) — an organization working together with one or more other organizations in a joint venture or similar arrangement.
Key fact — Figure 2.1, McDonald's Task Environment
  • Competitors: Burger King, Starbucks, Subway, Dairy Queen
  • Customers: individual consumers, institutional customers
  • Suppliers: Coca-Cola, wholesale food processors, packaging manufacturers
  • Regulators: FDA, SEC, EPA
  • Strategic partners: Walmart, Disney, foreign partners

C. The Internal Environment

Owners — whoever can claim property rights to an organization.
Board of directors — a governing body elected by a corporation's stockholders and charged with overseeing a firm's general management to ensure it is run to best serve the stockholders' interests.

Employees — a major element of the internal environment; workforce is becoming more diverse. Increased reliance on temporary workers ("temps") — individuals hired for short periods with no expectation of permanent employment.

Physical work environment — the actual physical environment and the work people do. Example: Pitney Bowes Credit Corporation redesigned its offices to resemble an indoor theme park.

Key fact — McDonald's has 1.02 billion shares of stock outstanding. Its board of directors has 14 members: 4 inside members (full-time executives) and 10 outside members.

II. The Ethical and Social Environment of Management

A. Individual Ethics in Organizations

Ethics — an individual's personal beliefs about whether a behavior, action, or decision is right or wrong. Note: people have ethics; organizations do not.
Ethical behavior — behavior that conforms to generally accepted social norms. Unethical behavior — behavior that does not conform to generally accepted social norms.
Managerial ethics — standards of behavior that guide individual managers in their work.

Three areas of managerial ethics: (1) treatment of employees by the organization (hiring/firing, wages, privacy); (2) treatment of the organization by employees — conflicts of interest (an employee's decision benefits the individual to the possible detriment of the organization), secrecy/confidentiality, and honesty; (3) the relationship between the firm/employees and other economic agents (customers, competitors, stockholders, suppliers, dealers, unions).

Key fact — Of roughly 300 pharmacists serving on state pharmacy boards, 72 were also employees of major drugstore/supermarket pharmacy chains — a potential conflict of interest.
Key fact — Hilton hired two Starwood executives who took eight boxes of documents related to Starwood's W hotels brand; Hilton's planned brand was to be called Denizen Hotels. Starwood sued after the theft was discovered.

B. Managing Ethical Behavior

Ethical leadership — leaders serve as role models, so their actions are subject to scrutiny. High-profile deposed executives named in the chapter: Mark Hurd (Hewlett-Packard), Dennis Kozlowski (Tyco), Kenneth Lay (Enron), Allen Stanford (Stanford Financial Group). Positive examples cited: Kenneth Chenault (American Express), James Sinegal (Costco).

Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 — a law requiring CEOs and CFOs to vouch personally for the truthfulness and fairness of their firms' financial disclosures; imposes tough measures to deter and punish corporate/accounting fraud.

Corporate governance failures cited: WorldCom's board approved a personal loan of $366 million to CEO Bernard Ebbers despite little evidence he could repay it; Tyco's board approved a $20 million bonus for one of its own members.

Codes of ethics — formal, written statements of the values and ethical standards that guide a firm's actions (e.g., Whirlpool, Texas Instruments, Hewlett-Packard). Infamous case: Enron's board voted to set aside the firm's code of ethics to implement a plan that violated it.

Ethics and information technology — privacy is a hot topic; both Facebook and Google were criticized for tracking users' movements online. Companies can post privacy policies and let users opt out of data collection.

C. Social Responsibility in Organizations

Social responsibility — the set of obligations an organization has to protect and enhance the societal context in which it functions.
Arguments FOR social responsibility (Figure 2.2)
  1. Business creates problems and should therefore help solve them.
  2. Corporations are citizens in our society.
  3. Business often has the resources necessary to solve problems.
  4. Business is a partner in our society, along with government and the general population.
Arguments AGAINST social responsibility (Figure 2.2)
  1. The purpose of business in U.S. society is to generate profit for owners.
  2. Involvement in social programs gives business too much power.
  3. There is potential for conflicts of interest.
  4. Business lacks the expertise to manage social programs.
Key fact — Economist Milton Friedman is specifically named as arguing that widening social responsibility undermines business's basic mission of earning profits for owners.

D. Managing Social Responsibility

Formal organizational dimensions:

Legal compliance — the extent to which the organization conforms to local, state, federal, and international laws.
Ethical compliance — the extent to which the organization's members follow basic ethical (and legal) standards of behavior.
Philanthropic giving — the awarding of funds or gifts to charities or other worthy causes.

Informal organizational dimensions: leadership, organizational culture, and how the organization responds to whistle-blowers.

Whistle-blowing — the disclosure, by an employee, of illegal or unethical conduct on the part of others within the organization.
Key fact — Harry Markopolos, a portfolio manager at Rampart Investments, spent nine years trying to convince the SEC that Bernard Madoff's firm was falsifying results. Bradley Birkenfeld provided evidence that Swiss bank UBS was evading corporate taxes; UBS was fined $780 million.
Key fact — When poisoned Tylenol appeared on shelves, Johnson & Johnson employees immediately pulled all packages without waiting for headquarters — reflecting a strong ethical/social-responsibility culture.

III. The International Environment of Management

A. Trends in International Business

Key fact — International trade volume increased more than 1,300 percent between 1960 and 2013. 184 of the world's largest corporations are headquartered in the U.S., versus 34 in Japan, 24 in France, 23 in China, and 36 in Britain.
Key fact — In 2012, ExxonMobil earned 67% of revenues and 61% of profits abroad; Avon earned 73% of revenues and 68% of profits abroad.

B. Levels of International Business Activity

Exporting — making a product in the firm's domestic marketplace and selling it in another country.
Importing — bringing a good, service, or capital into the home country from abroad.
Licensing — an arrangement whereby a firm allows another company to use its brand name, trademark, technology, patent, copyright, or other assets in exchange for a royalty based on sales. Franchising is a special form of licensing.
Strategic alliance — a cooperative arrangement between two or more firms for mutual gain. Joint venture — a special type of strategic alliance in which the partners actually share ownership of a new enterprise.
Direct investment — occurs when a firm headquartered in one country builds or purchases operating facilities or subsidiaries in a foreign country (the foreign operation becomes a wholly owned subsidiary).
Maquiladoras — light assembly plants built in Northern Mexico close to the U.S. border, given special tax breaks by the Mexican government.

C. The Context of International Business

Cultural environment — a country's culture includes all the values, symbols, beliefs, and language that guide behavior.

Key fact — In Saudi Arabia, few businesses offer towing services because Islamic teaching holds that people should not profit from others' misfortune; in Sudan, banks cannot pay or charge interest. Chevrolet's "Nova" sold poorly in Latin America because no va means "it doesn't go" in Spanish. Esso means "stalled car" in Japanese.

Controls on international trade:

Tariff — a tax collected on goods shipped across national boundaries.
Quota — a limit on the number or value of goods that can be traded. Example: Honda is allowed to import 425,000 autos per year into the U.S.
Export restraint agreements — accords in which governments voluntarily limit the volume or value of goods they export to or import from one another.

"Buy national" legislation — gives preference to domestic producers through content or price restrictions (e.g., Brazil requires Brazilian-made computers; Mexico requires 50% of car parts be made domestically).

Economic community — a set of countries that agree to markedly reduce or eliminate trade barriers among member nations.
European Union (EU) — traced to the 1957 Treaty of Rome (Belgium, France, Luxembourg, Germany, Italy, Netherlands); grew to 27 countries; adopted the common currency, the euro, beginning January 1, 2002.
North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) — an agreement among the United States, Canada, and Mexico to promote trade with one another.
General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) — a trade agreement intended to promote international trade by reducing trade barriers; first ratified in 1948 by 23 countries; by 1994, 117 countries had signed. Introduced the most favored nation (MFN) principle — preferential treatment given to one signatory must be extended to all signatories.
World Trade Organization (WTO) — came into existence January 1, 1995, replacing the GATT; headquartered in Geneva, Switzerland; ~140 member nations and 32 observer countries. Three basic goals: (1) promote trade flows via nondiscriminatory, predictable trade policies; (2) reduce remaining trade barriers through multilateral negotiations; (3) establish impartial procedures for resolving trade disputes.

IV. The Organization's Culture

Organizational culture — the set of values, beliefs, behaviors, customs, and attitudes that helps the organization's members understand what it stands for, how it does things, and what it considers important.

A. Importance of Organizational Culture

Culture determines the organization's "feel." No single universal culture works for all organizations. Examples: Microsoft (casual, long hours — stereotype), Bank of America (formal, rigid), Texas Instruments ("shirtsleeve" culture — no ties/jackets), Southwest Airlines (fun and excitement). Strong-culture companies (Starbucks, Procter & Gamble) tend to outperform weak-culture companies (Kmart).

B. Determinants of Organizational Culture

Culture typically develops over a long period, starting with the organization's founder. Example: James Cash Penney believed in treating employees/customers with respect — J.C. Penney employees are still called "associates" rather than employees. Corporate success and shared experiences also shape culture — e.g., Hallmark Cards' "Hallmark family" culture built from years of success; by contrast Kmart's culture is weak due to rapid management turnover.

C. Managing Organizational Culture

Managers must understand the current culture, then decide whether to maintain or change it. Culture can be maintained by rewarding/promoting people whose behavior fits the culture and by articulating it through slogans and ceremonies (e.g., Shell Oil's historical lobby display in Houston). Some cultures become dysfunctional — critics argue General Motors' culture over-emphasized internal divisional competition, contributing to its 2009 business crisis. Mergers can create culture clashes: Delta/Northwest merger caused conflict from differing cultures; when United and Continental merged, management wanted the new firm to adopt Continental's employee-friendly culture rather than United's culture of hostility/mistrust.

Chapter-Opening Case & Feature Boxes (may appear on exams)

Management in Action — Mercy Corps: Founded in 1979 as the "Save the Refugees Fund" (helping victims of famine/genocide in Cambodia); expanded and became Mercy Corps International in 1982. CEO quoted: Neal Keny-Guyer ("We function much as venture capitalists do... social VCs"). Has provided $2.2 billion in humanitarian aid across 114 countries, reaching ~19 million people in 36 nations annually. Three climate-program areas: alternative energy, sustainable resource management, and advocacy/outreach.
Sustainability Matters — HP Planet Partners: Began as companywide policy in 1991. Cartridges are processed through HP's Closed Loop System for Plastics Recycling at facilities in Nashville and Germany; plastic is shipped to the Lavergne Group in Montreal, Canada. Since 2005, HP has produced 500+ million new cartridges containing 60% recycled materials.
Tough Times, Tough Choices: Illustrates the economic dimension/business cycle — global economy boomed around 2005, the U.S. entered recession beginning in 2008, and the stock market/economy began recovering by 2012–2013.
